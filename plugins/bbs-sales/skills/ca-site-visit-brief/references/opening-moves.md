# Opening moves, and the phrasings that go wrong

## Lead with the price that went down

If any line has fallen since the last agreed price, that is the first sentence of the
meeting. It reframes the visit from "vendor wants something" to "we brought you money".

## Say the increase yourself, in one breath

Any line where we are now underwater, or where the price is rising, gets said before the
buyer finds it — but as **one** sentence carrying both the rise and the context:

> "Hard roll is the one item going up — the price we sold at in May was below today's cost.
> Here's where it lands for FY27, and it's still well below what this item goes for
> elsewhere in the state."

What not to do: split it across a "we're exposed here" bullet and a "we're strong here"
bullet. Both true, read as a contradiction.

**Never write, in any document that could be read over a shoulder, a phrase like "before
they find it".** It says out loud that the plan was to not mention it.

## Do not characterize a dip

Facilities buy in bursts. Compute the median non-zero month before calling anything a drop.

- Wrong: "Volume collapsed in July and August."
- Right: "What's queued for the fall?"

The wrong version invites "you're right, we moved it" — you have handed them the sentence.

## Certifications are not automatically a differentiator

In California the only certification BBS can lean on is DVBE — BBS is not a California
small business, so SB is never claimed here, and SDVOSB is the federal channel
(canonical-facts.md, "Certifications, by jurisdiction"). Before leaning on DVBE, check
whether the specific competitors on this account hold it too. If they do, the status is a supporting fact and the
argument has to be price, the buying mechanism, or service. Leaning on a certification the
competitor also has, in front of a buyer who knows that, costs credibility for the rest of
the meeting.

Never name a competitor or characterize their certifications in the room.

## Sell the mechanism, not the catalog

For a punch-out account, the strongest non-price lever is usually how they buy off-catalog
without a bid. One sentence, in their language:

> "Anything not in the catalog can still be bought on the NASPO contract through Grainger's
> Sourcing team — you don't go to bid, and it lands on a vehicle you already have."

[FLAG: confirm with Parker] — this sentence is not yet sourced to canon or to the LPA's own
terms. Until Parker confirms that off-catalog Grainger Sourcing orders land on the NASPO
contract without a bid for this buyer, do not say it in the room; ask the buyer how they
have handled off-catalog items before instead.

Pair it with a specific instance where this site already used it. Abstract capability is
forgettable; "you did this in August for the uniform shirts" is not.

## Do not add a vehicle to a working account

If the site buys 90-100% on one contract, do not introduce another. It solves a problem they
do not have and reads as a vendor optimizing for itself.

## Open the category they do not buy from us

The highest-value question on most visits is not about the category we already supply. Look
for a category we deliver at a sister facility and this site buys from someone else, then
ask who supplies it today. Tie it to something physical happening at the site — new beds, a
new wing, a program expansion.

## Ask the mechanical questions

Cheap, unglamorous, and they compound:

- Who else should be on the punch-out account?
- Who should quotes be addressed to, by name?
- What are the approval thresholds, and where does a purchase stop being a facility
  decision?

Frame the addressing question as improving *their* audit trail, never as fixing our filing.
